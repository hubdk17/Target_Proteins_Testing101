Project report · 1 November 2026

Designing a protein that sticks — and the question our pipeline never asks

A plain-language account of the binder design work: what we built, what it got right, where it is weak, and the one gap that turned out to be built into the design rather than a bug in any single tool.

Alphafold Architecture project · IL-6 lead case plus six other targets

If you only read one paragraph: we built a working three-step system that designs small new proteins aimed at a chosen patch on a target protein, and it produces designs that measurably depend on the one target residue we aimed them at. But no step in the system ever calculates how well the two surfaces actually fit together. What the last step measures is how confidently the computer can predict the pair as a structure. We have been using “predictable” as a stand-in for “binds.” Those are not the same thing, and closing that gap is the real opportunity.

Part 1What we are trying to do

Pick a disease-relevant protein. Pick the exact patch on its surface that matters — usually the patch it uses to talk to its partner. Then build a brand-new mini-protein, from scratch, whose job is to sit on that patch and block it.

“From scratch” is the important part. We are not borrowing an antibody or trimming down a natural partner. The computer invents a shape and a sequence that have never existed. This is called de novo binder design.

Three words used throughout

  • Target — the protein we want to block. Fixed, we do not change it.
  • Binder — the new mini-protein we are designing. About 40 to 55 building blocks long.
  • Hotspot — a specific residue on the target that we tell the software to aim the binder at.

Part 2The three steps

The pipeline is a relay. Each step hands its output to the next, and — this matters later — no step can send work back.

Step 1 · RFdiffusion

Draw a shape

Given the target and the hotspots, it sculpts a backbone — the folded path of the new binder — pressed against that patch. Shape only. No sequence yet.

Step 2 · ProteinMPNN

Write a sequence into that shape

Given the shape from step 1, it chooses which residue goes in each position so the binder will actually fold that way.

Step 3 · AlphaFold2

Score the pair

It predicts the structure of binder and target together and reports how confident it is about the contact region. Low uncertainty passes the gate; high uncertainty is discarded.

We ran this end to end, scored thousands of candidates, and audited each step with its own experiment. The rest of this report is what each audit showed.

Part 3The test case: IL-6

IL-6 is an inflammation signalling protein and a genuine drug target. We work from a crystal structure of it, cleaned up to a single chain of 157 residues. We aim at the patch called site III, and within that patch one residue dominates: a tryptophan at position 157, Trp157. Tryptophan is the largest residue in the alphabet, and a big flat greasy one — exactly the kind of feature a designed surface can grip.

Trp157 became our measuring stick. If our designs are real, removing Trp157 should hurt them. If they are an artefact of the scoring, removing it should change nothing. We come back to that in Part 6.

We also prepared seven other targets — among them PD-L1, PCSK9, BCL-2, caspase-3, BRCA1 and GLP-1R — so that nothing we concluded would rest on one protein.

Part 4Step 1: what the shape generator does and does not know

Aiming works, and it works well

Without hotspots, the software puts a binder somewhere plausible on the target, but not necessarily where we want it. With hotspots named, aim improves sharply: in one comparison, designs touching site III went from 2 out of 10 to 7 out of 7.

But it has no sense of whether a shape is a sensible protein

Asked for a fixed-length binder with no pressure to be compact, roughly half the output is a single straight helix — a rod. A rod laid along a surface racks up plenty of contacts, so if you rank candidates by contact count you will select rods preferentially. They are not plausible mini-proteins.

The fix is to treat compactness as a pass/fail gate applied first, not a tiebreaker applied last: measure how spread out the design is relative to what a globular protein of that length should be, throw out anything too stretched, and only then look at contacts.

A result we had to walk back

We initially believed a particular compactness nudge in the software was a general improvement. It is not. It helped substantially at one site, did nothing at another, and slightly hurt a third. It is site-dependent, and we no longer generalise it. This is the first of two occasions where we generalised from one protein and were wrong.

The hotspot-count experiment: six targets, 300 designs per arm

A real question with an actionable answer: does naming more hotspots give better designs? We ran every one of six targets twice — once with one hotspot, once with five — fifty designs in every cell, everything else identical. The readout is the share of designs that pass the compactness gate.

Pooled, that is a clear effect and not a fluke. But the average hides the shape of it. Almost all the gain is in the targets that were failing. The two already working at around 40% barely moved. More aiming information rescues a badly aimed run; it does not lift a run that is already aimed well.

The genuinely interesting part: one number, two causes

BCL-2, caspase-3 and BRCA1 all sat on the floor at 0–6%. We had a tidy story for why: all three naturally bind a long stretched-out piece of another protein, so a compact 40–55mer cannot reach. That story was wrong for two of them — BCL-2 and caspase-3 jumped to the middle of the pack the moment they were aimed properly. They were not hard targets. They were badly aimed.

BRCA1 stayed on the floor, its typical design still rod-shaped, and adding more designs produced no additional passes. So the explanation survives — for BRCA1 alone. It has a second problem too: the patch it recognises is read through a chemically modified residue that a designed protein cannot present at all.

Four targets that looked identically hopeless were three setup mistakes and one real limit. Only running the proper comparison told them apart.

Worth recording: we wrote down our prediction before reading this out — “everything moves within about ten points, ordering preserved.” Two targets moved 24 and 28 points and crossed from bottom to middle. Prediction wrong, for the same reason as before: generalising from one site.

Part 5Step 2: it reads the target’s chemistry, and it is already too late

We asked a direct question. Hold the shape and the random seed fixed, change exactly one residue on the target surface, and re-run sequence design. Does the output change?

It changes a lot. Between 22 and 24 of 24 sequences differed on every shape we tried, with 8 to 11 positions out of 40–55 rewritten — from a single surface mutation on the target.

So the pipeline does contain a step that reads the target’s chemistry, and reads it strongly. The problem is when. By the time step 2 runs, step 1 has already committed the geometry without any energetic assessment of the fit, and step 2 cannot reject a shape. It can only write the best possible sequence for a shape it was handed. This is the sharpest form of our central point.

A mistake that cost us real time

We also checked whether the sequences step 2 writes actually fold into the shape they were written for. They do — 85% of a 376-sequence pool fold back to within a close tolerance. But an early batch of downstream work had been built on one sequence that happened to be one of only two bad folders in its group, and a confidence number inside that group turned out to be inverted. Several conclusions had to be re-derived. The lesson we now apply everywhere: sample several sequences per shape, or do not judge the shape. Within a single shape, scores vary by more than the whole width of our pass/fail gate, so a passing score belongs to the sequence, never to the shape.

Part 6Step 3: the gate, and the one experiment that mattered most

What the gate is

We predict the binder and target together and read one number: the predicted uncertainty in the contact region. Below about 10 passes. We use this specific number for one reason only — it is the one threshold in the whole pipeline that has been calibrated against roughly a thousand designs actually tested in a laboratory. Everything else in our setup is a judgement call; this is not.

Two honest caveats we repeat every time. First, the score is not reproducible to the decimal — the same input on the same machine can move by nearly 3 units, and different hardware shifts scores by several percent. A single design needs a large gap to be meaningful; only an average over roughly nine designs is tight. Any number sitting within about 1 of the gate is not a verdict. Second, the gate is good at separating good from bad but poor at ranking bad designs against each other, because the score saturates.

Do the designs actually need Trp157?

This is the experiment the whole project was working toward, and it is a clean one. Take the nine designs that passed. Score each one three times:

  • Normal — the target as it is.
  • Trp157 removed — the residue we aimed at, deleted to a small stub.
  • Control — a different residue removed, one we measured as sitting 17 to 23 Å away from every design. Far outside the contact patch. Nothing should happen.

The result was unambiguous.

+8.01
score penalty from removing Trp157 — worse in 9 of 9 designs
+0.14
penalty from the distant control residue — worse in only 4 of 9, i.e. noise

About sixty times apart, and the two arms differ by one residue. The designs are not generic sticky blobs; they depend on the specific feature we aimed them at. Separately, we measured the footprint of all 17 top designs and found Trp157 in close contact in every single one.

One design family behaved differently and we are keeping it: it lost only a little when Trp157 was removed, and it makes fewer contacts with it. That looks like a second way of binding the same patch, not leaning on that residue. Worth following up rather than discarding.

Part 7The finding that is about the architecture, not the tools

At no point does the pipeline calculate how well the binder’s surface and the target’s surface fit each other. There is no packing, no charge interaction, no accounting for the water that has to be pushed out of the way.

Walk it through once more with that in mind:

  • Step 1 does receive the target’s real sequence — we verified this in the source code, after previously claiming the opposite. But identity arrives as a bare label per residue, with no side-chain geometry and no chemistry, and the hotspot instruction is literally one bit per residue: aim here, yes or no. It commits the geometry on that.
  • Step 2 reads the chemistry properly, and cannot act on it — the geometry is already fixed and it has no veto.
  • Step 3 reports prediction confidence. A confident prediction says the pair is easy to model. It does not say the pair is held together.

So “this complex is predictable” is standing in for “this complex binds.” That substitution is a property of how the pipeline is assembled, not a defect in RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN or AlphaFold individually. Each tool does its own job well.

It also fits what the field sees in practice: a method that scores predictability will hand you many confident designs and few real binders. We flag that as interpretation consistent with the literature, not something we measured ourselves.

Part 8What is missing, stated precisely

The tempting conclusion is “the model needs more information.” We think that is the wrong diagnosis. The information is present — step 2 proves the target’s chemistry is readable and consequential. What is missing is a representation of how two surfaces fit together, available at the moment the geometry is chosen.

In our words: the pipeline needs target-conditioned interface features during shape generation. Not a wider general-purpose input channel. Concretely, the single most obvious change is to give hotspot conditioning some chemistry — today it is one yes/no bit per residue, which is a targeting instrument and nothing more.

And a second, cheaper change: add a real energetic check as an extra gate after step 3, so that “predictable” and “favourable” are two separate tests a design has to pass. That work is underway.

Part 9How we try not to fool ourselves

Several of the corrections in this report were caught by the same handful of habits, and they are worth stating because they are the reason the Trp157 result is believable.

  • Write the predicted number down before measuring. This caught two of our own wrong generalisations.
  • Design checks that can fail. Our recurring failure mode is a check that was never capable of returning bad news — searching a log for a value the program never writes, and reading the silence as confirmation.
  • Verify what arrived, not what was sent. Five hotspots once silently became one, because a list was truncated in transit. The whole pipeline ran, happily, on the wrong input.
  • Run the negative control in the same batch. Not later, not on different hardware.
  • Treat any value identical across independent samples as a bug, not as stability.
  • Reading the source tells you what the code does, never which part drives an effect. Only a multi-arm experiment does. We confirmed this the hard way: a target mutation’s entire effect turned out to run through the residue’s name, not through the structural detail the code reads.
  • Check whether a comparison had the power to detect anything before believing a null result.

One structural-biology trap deserves its own mention. A standard structure-repair tool rebuilt a missing loop on one of our targets using five mirror-image residues — chemically impossible in a natural protein, one of them right at the interface. None of our quality checks could see it, because none of them looked. We now test handedness explicitly, everywhere a structure gets rebuilt or hydrogens get added.

Part 10What happens next

  1. A real energy calculation on the passing designs, as a second independent gate beside the confidence score. Predictions written down in advance, as usual.
  2. Molecular dynamics on a passing design and a failing one from the same shape, run side by side. This tests whether a complex holds together, which is not the same as affinity — and one arm alone would tell us nothing.
  3. A positive control. A published, laboratory-validated binder against PD-L1 run through our pipeline unchanged. If our gate cannot recognise a design known to work, the gate is mis-calibrated and we need to know that before trusting it further.
  4. The length experiment on BRCA1 only — longer binders for the one target where “it cannot reach” still stands.
  5. Visual inspection of the top designs on the site III patch, which is already set up.

Glossary

Backbone
The folded path of a protein chain, without the chemical groups hanging off it. Step 1 produces one of these.
Residue
One building block of a protein. A protein is a chain of them; each has its own chemistry.
Site III / patch
The named region of a target’s surface we are aiming at.
Interaction energy
A calculation of how favourable it is for two surfaces to be touching. The thing no step in our pipeline computes.
The gate
A pass/fail threshold on the step-3 confidence score. It is the only threshold in our pipeline calibrated against laboratory results.
Negative control
A version of an experiment where nothing should happen. If something does, your measurement is picking up the wrong thing.
