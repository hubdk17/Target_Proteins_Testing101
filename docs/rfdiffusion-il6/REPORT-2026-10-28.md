# De novo binder design against IL-6: what we built, what the mathematics says, and where the architecture leaks

**Project report, 28 October 2026.**
Target: human interleukin-6, PDB **1ALU**, 1.90 Å.
Compute: Thapar AI Foundry, 3 nodes × 8 GPUs.
Status of the central question: **open, and now for the right reason.**

This report has three parts, deliberately separated.

1. **Conceptually, from the basics.** What we are trying to do and how the
   machinery works, with no mathematics.
2. **Mathematically.** Every number we rely on, with the formula that produced
   it, so each claim can be checked or refuted.
3. **Faults and architectural gaps.** What is wrong with RFdiffusion, with

# Part 1 — Conceptually, from the basics

## 1.1 What the goal is

A **protein binder** is a small protein designed to stick tightly and
specifically to one chosen spot on one chosen target protein. If you can make
them to order, you have a general tool: block a signal, tag a cell type, carry a
drug.

Our target is **interleukin-6 (IL-6)**, a signalling protein central to
inflammation. It is a bundle of four long helices. It works by touching three
partner proteins at three different patches of its surface, called sites I, II
and III. We aim at **site III**, and in particular at one residue, **tryptophan
157**, which contributes roughly a quarter of the buried surface when site III
engages its partner. Trp157 is the single most important residue in that patch.

So the concrete goal: design a small protein, around 40 residues, that grips
IL-6 at site III.

## 1.2 Why this is hard, in one paragraph

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a specific three-dimensional
shape. Two things must be true at once for a designed binder to work. It must
**fold by itself** into the shape you intended, and that shape must be
**complementary** to the target's surface, so the two fit together with no gaps
and with chemically compatible surfaces touching. These are separate
requirements and they fight each other: the greasy residues that pay for binding
are the same ones a small protein wants to bury inside itself to fold.

## 1.3 The pipeline, as a factory line

The standard modern approach runs three tools in sequence. The clearest way to
see it is as a factory line where each station is blind to something.

### Stage 1 — RFdiffusion: make a shape

RFdiffusion is a **generative model of protein backbones**. "Backbone" means the
load-bearing chain only: for each residue, the nitrogen, the alpha carbon, the
carbonyl carbon and its oxygen. No side chains at all. Every residue in the
output is a glycine placeholder.

The analogy that holds: it bends a length of wire into a shape that sits nicely
against your target. It is not choosing what the beads on the wire are made of.

You give it the target's structure and a short list of **hotspot residues** you
want the binder to touch, and you tell it how long the binder should be. It
returns a backbone.

### Stage 2 — ProteinMPNN: write the sequence

ProteinMPNN takes a fixed backbone and asks: *which amino acid sequence would
most likely fold into this exact shape?* It reads the geometry and writes a
sequence onto it.

Now the beads get their materials. Crucially, ProteinMPNN **cannot change the
wire**. It can only pick the best materials for the shape it was handed. If the
shape is bad, the honest output is a bad sequence, and we have seen exactly that.

### Stage 3 — the filter: predict the complex and score it

Most designs fail. So the third station is an exam. You take the designed
sequence, hand it to a structure-prediction model together with the target's
sequence, and ask it to predict the two chains together. Then you read off how
**confident** the model is about the interface between them, and rank designs by
that confidence.

This is the step that decides what gets made in a laboratory. It is also the
step we have proved is broken in our configuration, and that is the single most
important result in this report.

## 1.4 The one idea to carry away

**Confidence is not affinity.** The filter does not compute how strongly two
proteins stick together. It computes how sure a prediction model is about where
the atoms go. Those two quantities are related, but they come apart precisely
where designed interfaces are concerned, and nothing in the pipeline ever
computes the first one.

## 1.5 What we actually have, in plain terms

We have one design carried the whole way through, called **`short_9`**: a
40-residue protein made of two helices packed against each other, a shape called
a helical hairpin. It is the right architecture for a protein this size.
ProteinMPNN gave it a proper greasy core, and when we ask a model to fold it on
its own it is very confident, which is the best single number in the project.
Behind it sit 60 more backbones that pass our quality gate and have never been
through stages 2 and 3.

We have **no evidence that any of it binds IL-6.** Nothing has been made in a
laboratory, and the computational filter that was supposed to tell us cannot
currently tell a designed binder from a random peptide of the same length.

## 2.1 How the target's chemistry reaches stage 1

Read directly out of `rfdiffusion/inference/model_runners.py`:

```
454:  seq_t = torch.full((1, L_mapped), 21)                       # all mask token
455:  seq_t[contig_map.hal_idx0] = seq_orig[contig_map.ref_idx0]  # real sequence copied in
461:  seq_t[~self.mask_seq.squeeze()] = 21                        # re-mask the diffused part
462:  seq_t = one_hot(seq_t, num_classes=22)                      # [L, 22]
683:  if self.preprocess_conf.d_t1d >= 24:                        # hotspot block
697:      hotspot_tens[hotspot_idx] = 1.0
```

So for a complex of mapped length $L$, the target's sequence enters as

$$S \in \{0,1\}^{L \times 22}, \qquad \sum_{c=1}^{22} S_{ic} = 1 ,$$

and the hotspot signal enters as a single extra channel

$$h \in \{0,1\}^{L}, \qquad h_i = 1 \iff i \in \text{hotspots}.$$

**Information content of the emphasis signal: exactly 1 bit per residue.** The
model is told that residue 157 is a tryptophan and that it matters. It is told
nothing about where the indole rings point, what volume they sweep, or what
would pack against them.

## 2.2 The compactness gate

For $N$ backbone points at positions $\mathbf r_i$ with centroid
$\bar{\mathbf r}$, the **radius of gyration** is

$$R_g = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \lVert \mathbf r_i - \bar{\mathbf r}\rVert^2 }.$$

A folded globular protein of $N$ residues has, empirically,

$$R_g^{\text{compact}} \approx 2.2\, N^{0.38} \ \text{Å},$$

so we score a design by the dimensionless ratio

$$\rho = \frac{R_g^{\text{measured}}}{2.2\,N^{0.38}}, \qquad \text{gate: } \rho \le 1.30 .$$

$\rho$ is **length-free by construction**, which is why it is the only stage-1
criterion that survived every later audit.

Worked example, `short_9`: $N = 40$, $R_g = 10.8$ Å.
$2.2 \times 40^{0.38} = 2.2 \times 4.063 = 8.94$, so $\rho = 10.8/8.94 = 1.21$. Passes.

### The straight-helix signature, derived

For $N$ equally spaced collinear points at spacing $d$, put
$x_i = \left(i - \frac{N+1}{2}\right)d$. Then

$$R_g^2 = \frac{d^2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(i-\tfrac{N+1}{2}\Big)^2
        = \frac{d^2}{N}\cdot\frac{N(N^2-1)}{12}
        = \frac{d^2(N^2-1)}{12},$$

$$\boxed{\,R_g = d\sqrt{\frac{N^2-1}{12}}\,}$$

For $N=50$ and an $\alpha$-helical rise of $d = 1.5$ Å:
$R_g = 1.5\sqrt{208.25} = 1.5 \times 14.43 = \mathbf{21.65}$ Å.

**Measured mean across 44 designs: 21.62 Å, SD 0.023.** The agreement to three
significant figures is the proof that those designs are single straight
helices, and that their suspiciously invariant $R_g$ was geometry rather than a
bug. $\rho = 21.65/(2.2\times 50^{0.38}) = 21.65/9.80 = 2.21$, which is the
signature to recognise.

## 2.3 Hotspot conditioning also buys compactness

Jobs **172779** (hotspots) and **172780** (none), 150 designs each, binder
length fixed at exactly 50 in both arms, no ROG potential.

| arm | pass $\rho \le 1.30$ | rate |
|---|---|---|
| hotspots `A47,A155,A157,A158` | 47/150 | 31.3% |
| no hotspots | 13/150 | 8.7% |

Two-proportion $z$-test with pooled $\hat p = 60/300 = 0.200$:

$$\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\tfrac{1}{n_1}+\tfrac{1}{n_2}\right)}
= \sqrt{0.200 \times 0.800 \times \tfrac{2}{150}} = 0.0462,$$

$$z = \frac{0.3133 - 0.0867}{0.0462} = \mathbf{4.91}, \qquad p \approx 10^{-6}.$$

Same test on the rod rate ($\rho \ge 2.05$), 76/150 against 122/150:
$\hat p = 0.660$, SE $= 0.0547$, $z = (0.8133-0.5067)/0.0547 = \mathbf{5.61}$.

**Claude predicted both arms near 20% and hotspots irrelevant to compactness.
Wrong on both counts.** An earlier version of this comparison at $n=30$ gave
6/30 against 5/30 and was read as "indistinguishable, as expected" — which was
a statement about statistical power reported as though it were about biology.

## 2.4 Interface measurement on a side-chain-free backbone

RFdiffusion output has no side chains, so all-atom distance criteria do not
apply. Calibrated empirically on run 1:

| criterion | residues found | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| CA–CA < 12 Å | 83 | reaches into the core |
| all-atom < 5 Å | 3 | nothing bridges the gap without side chains |
| **CA–CA < 10 Å** | **58** | right scale for a footprint |
| **CA–CA < 8 Å** | 21 | real grip rather than grazing |

Compare designs by **grip**, $g = c_8 / N$, not by raw counts. Example:
`short_7`, $c_8 = 16$, $N = 53$, $g = 0.30$.

**A limit worth stating:** 10 Å CA–CA is wider than a helix is thick, so for a
helix lying on a surface *both* of its faces fall inside the cutoff. The method
has no power to say which face points at the target.

## 2.5 ProteinMPNN, and what its score is

ProteinMPNN factorises the sequence likelihood given a fixed backbone $X$ in
some decoding order $\pi$:

$$p(s \mid X) = \prod_{k=1}^{N} p\big(s_{\pi(k)} \mid X,\, s_{\pi(1)},\dots,s_{\pi(k-1)}\big),$$

and the reported score is the mean negative log-likelihood per residue,

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k} \log p\big(s_{\pi(k)} \mid \cdot\big),
\qquad \text{lower is better.}$$

Measured on `short_9`: $\mathcal{L} \in [1.30, 1.37]$ over 8 samples, against
**2.51** for the poly-glycine input. Middling rather than excellent.

The consensus over 8 independent samples:

| position | 3 | 7 | 11 | 17 | 26 | 30 | 34 | 38 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| residue | Y (8/8) | L/M (8/8) | V (8/8) | I/L/Y (8/8) | L | L | L | L |

Positions 3, 7, 11 are $i,\,i{+}4,\,i{+}8$ — one face of an $\alpha$-helix,
since a helix turns every 3.6 residues. Positions 26, 30, 34, 38 are the same
pattern on a second helix. Eight stochastic samples agreeing there is not
coincidence: it is a located hydrophobic core, two amphipathic helices burying
their greasy faces against each other.

**`seq_recovery = 0.0000` is meaningless here** and must not be quoted. The
"native" sequence of an RFdiffusion output is poly-glycine, so recovery against
it is zero by construction.

## 2.6 The scoring stage, and the measurement that broke it

The confidence metrics. Predicted aligned error $\mathrm{PAE}_{ij}$ is the
model's expected positional error of residue $j$ when the prediction is
superposed on residue $i$. From the same head,

$$\mathrm{pTM} = \max_{\text{align}} \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i} \frac{1}{1+\left(\hat e_i/d_0(L)\right)^2},$$

and **ipTM** restricts the sum to residue pairs in *different* chains. So both
are functions of a predicted error distribution, not of an energy.

### The eight-condition ladder, job 172783, 50 diffusion samples per condition

Standard error of a mean, $\mathrm{SEM} = \sigma/\sqrt n$. At $n=5$ the earlier
run gave $\mathrm{SEM} = 0.116/\sqrt5 = 0.052$, too coarse to resolve a
0.05–0.15 effect, which is why $n$ was raised to 50 ($\mathrm{SEM} \approx 0.022$).

| condition | ipTM | SD | $\Delta$ vs wt | Welch $t$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `wt` (the design) | 0.466 | 0.157 | — | — |
| **`random40`** | **0.502** | 0.161 | **+0.036** | **+1.13** |
| `k120a` | 0.414 | 0.195 | −0.052 | −1.46 |
| `w157a` | 0.394 | 0.097 | −0.072 | −2.76 |
| `alaif` (dead interface) | 0.393 | 0.146 | −0.073 | −2.40 |
| `e93a` (chosen as irrelevant) | 0.376 | 0.143 | **−0.090** | **−3.03** |
| `targetonly` | — | — | pLDDT 0.667 ± 0.090 | |
| `binderonly` | — | — | pLDDT **0.905 ± 0.028** | |

Welch's statistic, with the wt–random40 pair worked out:

$$t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}
= \frac{0.502-0.466}{\sqrt{0.157^2/50 + 0.161^2/50}}
= \frac{0.036}{0.0318} = +1.13 .$$

**Three readings, in order of importance.**

1. **The gate failed.** A random 40-mer scored *above* the design, and the
   deliberately destroyed interface scored *below* the random peptide. A metric
   that ranks a random peptide first cannot rank designs.
2. **The controls prevented a false positive.** `w157a` at $t=-2.76$ would have
   read as a result on its own. But `e93a`, chosen precisely because it should
   be irrelevant, moved *most*. The model responds to any perturbation of the
   target sequence, not to interface chemistry.
3. **The mechanism is target instability.** Interface confidence tracks
   whole-complex pLDDT at $r = +0.78$, and `targetonly` shows IL-6 alone reaches
   only $0.667 \pm 0.090$ from sequence — a 1.90 Å crystal structure we handed
   the model. A point mutation makes IL-6 harder to fold, complex confidence
   falls, interface confidence follows.

Two statistical cautions. `complex_ipde` against ipTM has $r = -0.854$, so they
are **not independent readouts** and must not be cited as two pieces of
evidence. And `pair_chains_iptm` is **not symmetric**: $[1][0]$ equals `iptm`
exactly while $[0][1]$ is systematically lower, so the two must not be averaged.

## 2.7 The one clean positive result

$$\text{pLDDT}(\texttt{binderonly}) = 0.905 \pm 0.028 \quad (n=50)$$

Highest mean and smallest spread in the entire project. `short_9` folds on its
own, so its conserved hydrophobics are not purely interface-facing. This
resolves, in the design's favour, the stage-2 worry that the greasy residues
might all point at the target leaving no core.

# Part 3 — Faults and architectural gaps

Ordered within each stage by how much the fault costs us.

## 3.1 RFdiffusion

**F1. Side-chain identity arrives impoverished, and the emphasis signal is one bit.**
*Corrected 28 October: this project assumed for five weeks that the target's
chemistry was discarded here entirely. It is not — line 455 copies the real
sequence in.* What is actually missing is side-chain **geometry** and
**chemistry**. A 22-way one-hot says "tryptophan"; it does not say where the
rings point or what would pack against them, and $h_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the whole
of "this residue matters". **This is the project's technical gap, stated
correctly, and it is the defensible form of the original wide-representation
hypothesis: the capacity worth adding is in the target-conditioned interface
channel, not in MSA width.**
*Status:* whether the output responds to a target point mutation has **never
been measured**. It is now a real experiment: 50 designs against wild-type IL-6
and 50 against a W157A target PDB, identical settings, compare distributions.

**F2. Foldability is not an objective.** Fix a length, add no compactness
pressure, and one long straight helix is the model's cheapest answer:
**176 of 300** length-50 designs sit at $\rho \approx 2.2$. The fix exists in
the package and we did not use it —
`potentials.guiding_potentials=["type:binder_ROG,weight:3"]`. It is
load-bearing, not a refinement: the shortcut turned $n=30$ into $n=6$.

**F3. Hotspot conditioning silently doubles as a compactness prior** (31% against
9%, $z=4.9$). Useful, but it means two things you would want to control
independently are entangled, and nothing documents it. Competing explanation
still open: site III may simply be a curved patch that suits hairpins, testable
by repeating on a flatter site.

**F4. Fixed binder length may itself cause the rod failure mode** (§2.8,
suggestive only).

**F5. No interaction energy.** A generative model of shape has no energy
function between the chains at all.

**F6. The mapped-tensor order differs from the written contig order.** The
script writes the target first; the log prints the masked binder first.
Unexplained. It does not propagate, because every downstream step reads the
output PDB, where chain assignment was verified directly. Recorded because this
project has twice been bitten by ordering assumptions that looked harmless.

## 3.2 ProteinMPNN

**F7. It can see the target's chemistry and cannot act on it.** The fixed chain's
real sequence is supplied, so a target mutation *is* visible. But it cannot
reject a backbone — only write the best sequence for the one it was handed, and
that backbone was chosen by a model that held the chemistry as a bare one-hot
and may never have used it. **By the time anything in the pipeline has the
chemistry in a usable form, the decision that mattered has been taken.**

**F8. It has never been audited, and it is the cheapest audit available.** This
is the one standard stage that both *can* see W157A and is actually used, and
nobody has tested whether its output responds. Run MPNN twice on the identical
`short_9` backbone, chain A wild type against chain A with W157 mutated to
alanine, 48 sequences each, and compare residue preferences at the positions
contacting 157. About a minute on a GPU, 11 minutes on CPU.
*Either answer is a result.* No response gives a clean architectural claim that
no stage sees a target point mutation. A response means the sensitivity exists
and is being discarded downstream, which is more fixable and more interesting.

**F9. Its objective is not binding.** $\mathcal{L}$ is a sequence likelihood
given a backbone. That is not binding free energy and is not monotonic in it.

**F10. No side-chain geometry either.** It encodes backbone atoms, so it knows a
residue is a tryptophan without knowing where that tryptophan's rings actually
point.

**A virtue worth recording.** Poly-alanine output is an *honest diagnostic*: it
means the backbone has no hydrophobic core, nothing to bury, so the model picks
the small residue that cannot clash. We used it as a measurement and it was
right.

## 3.3 The scoring stage

**F11. It measures predictability, not complementarity, and we have proved it**
(§2.6). This is the costliest fault in the project, because everything
downstream of the filter — which designs to make, whether a mutation matters —
is unsupported until it is fixed.

**F12. Target drift.** In single-sequence mode the model cannot reliably fold
IL-6 itself, so the worst samples score an interface against a mis-folded
target. $r = +0.78$ between interface and whole-complex confidence.

**F13. It responds to any target perturbation, not to interface chemistry.**
`e93a`, chosen to be irrelevant, moved the score most.

**F14. Boltz has no published binder-filter calibration.** So 0.466 has no
threshold to be compared against, and AlphaFold's published thresholds do not
transfer to it. This matters for deciding what to synthesise and matters less
for asking whether a metric *moves* against its own noise floor.

**F15. Two metrics that look independent are not** ($r = -0.854$), and one
matrix that looks symmetric is not.

## 3.4 The pipeline as a whole

**F16. No stage computes an interaction energy.** Not one of the three evaluates
packing, electrostatics or desolvation between the two chains. **Confidence that
a complex is predictable stands in for evidence that it binds.** This is a
property of the architecture, not a bug in any one tool, and it is the central
claim of the project.

**F17. The ordering destroys information.** The shape is committed at stage 1,
before any stage holds the target's chemistry in a form it can act on. A
side-chain-resolved rescoring step between stages 1 and 2, cheap enough for
hundreds of backbones, is the natural remedy.

**F18. The stage 2 to stage 3 handoff is manual and has corrupted data twice.**
ProteinMPNN emits sequences; the scorer wants structures. Threading one onto the
other by hand produced two silently broken files, both from off-by-one errors in
fixed-column arithmetic, and in both cases the checks written alongside passed
for the wrong reason.

**F19. Nothing is calibrated on this target.** No measured affinity exists for
IL-6 site III binders at a scale any model could learn from. This bounds how
much target-specific capacity is worth adding before overfitting, and it is the
standing constraint on the whole research direction.

**F20. One design, end to end.** Every stage-2 and stage-3 number here comes
from a single backbone. 60 more pass the gate and are untouched.

## 3.5 Engineering faults found, which are findings in their own right

These cost real time and each one is now a rule.

| fault | what happened | the rule |
|---|---|---|
| Uninitialised awk subscript | `bx[nb]` filled before `nb` set; an unset subscript is `""`, so loops began at `j=0` and read a **phantom atom at the origin in both chains**. Three rounds of conclusions rested on it. | `BEGIN{na=0;nb=0}`. Treat any quantity invariant across independent samples as a bug until proven otherwise. |
| Unmapped lookup | A table built `for(i=1;i<=38;i+=2)` over 40 fields never mapped `Y → TYR`; tyrosine lines were written **three characters short, shifting every coordinate column**. Two of three checks passed for the wrong reason. | Make a lookup miss loud (`if(n=="") exit 1`). Prefer an **integrity** check to a content check: one unique `length($0)` proves nothing shifted. |
| Exit code, direction 1 | Job 172280 reported FAILED when the work had succeeded; the exit code came from a trailing diagnostic `cat` on a stale path. | Do not let a diagnostic be the last command, or put it behind `|| true`. |
| Exit code, direction 2 | Job 174205 reported COMPLETED `0:0` with **7 of 8 conditions dead**. | Read success off the **output file count** and the **elapsed time against a known rate**. 2:14 against a 23-minute floor was the tell. |
| Error handling defeated | Boltz catches a bad input, prints `Skipping`, exits 0 — so our own `|| echo "CONDITION FAILED"` never fired. `grep -c` returned 0 while seven conditions failed. | A per-input tool's error handling can hide failure from your wrapper's. Count outputs. |
| Guessed a schema twice | Two guesses at why the Boltz template failed were wrong; the file was already correct on both counts. | Read the schema. Then **delete the failing field** rather than guess its right value. |
| Chain-order convention | `predict.py` slices by `binderlen` and treats `[:binderlen]` as the binder, so **the binder must be the first chain**. RFdiffusion writes the target first. Submitted as-is it would have inverted the mask that pins the target and produced a full score file with no error. | Caught by reading the code before submitting. Check a convention, do not assume it. |

# The claim, bounded

**What we can say.** In the configuration measured, a co-folding interface
confidence score cannot distinguish a designed 40-residue binder from a random
peptide of the same length, and responds to target point mutations in proportion
to how much they destabilise the target rather than to whether they sit at the
interface. Stage 1 receives the target's sequence but only as a bare one-hot
with one bit of emphasis, and whether it acts on it is unmeasured. No stage of
the pipeline computes an interaction energy.

**What we cannot say.** That co-folding models cannot filter binders. That is
one design, one model, one configuration, and a configuration now identified as
broken in a specific way. We also cannot say anything at all about whether
`short_9` binds IL-6.

**What this is.** A diagnosis of a filter and an audit of an architecture, not a
claim about a molecule.
